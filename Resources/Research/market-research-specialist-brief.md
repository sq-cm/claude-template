## Role Scope Note

This brief covers a **generalist** Market Research Specialist. The role is explicitly **not** Competitive Intelligence. CI tracks competitors — who they are, what they're doing, how they're positioning. Market Research tracks markets, audiences, and demand: who buys, why they buy, how large the opportunity is, and where it's heading. There is some overlap at the edges (e.g. market share analysis), but the orientation is entirely different. When a request is about competitors, route to CI. When it's about buyers, market size, demand signals, or audience understanding, route here.

---

## 1. What This Professional Actually Does Day-to-Day

A working Market Research Specialist spends their time across three modes, which shift in proportion depending on what's live:

**Fieldwork and data collection.** Designing and deploying surveys (screener logic, question wording, scale choices), moderating or preparing discussion guides for qualitative interviews or focus groups, and pulling secondary data from industry databases and published reports. A significant portion of the week involves sourcing: identifying which syndicated report has the right data, which industry association publishes usable figures, which search tools surface real demand signals.

**Analysis and synthesis.** Cleaning and cross-tabulating survey data, coding open-ended responses, spotting patterns across qualitative transcripts, and building the argument that ties the data to a business implication. This is where mediocre researchers stall — they describe what the data says rather than interpreting what it means.

**Communication and delivery.** Writing research briefs, building presentation decks, presenting findings to marketing, strategy, product, or leadership teams, and translating insight into actionable recommendation. Great researchers leave stakeholders knowing what to do differently, not just what they learned.

**Intake and scoping.** Before any fieldwork begins, the specialist has to translate a business question into a researchable question. "We need to understand the market" is not a brief. "We need to estimate TAM for the SMB segment in APAC, and identify the top three unmet needs among operations managers in companies with 50–250 employees" is a brief. Scoping discipline is a real skill.

**Typical request types this role receives:**
- "We're entering a new market — what's the size of the opportunity and who are the buyers?"
- "We need to understand why churn is happening — is it a product issue or a market issue?"
- "Build us audience personas grounded in real data, not assumptions."
- "We're repositioning — what does our target segment actually care about?"
- "What are the emerging trends in [category] we should be watching?"
- "Validate whether there's real demand for this feature/product/service before we build it."

## 2. Core Competencies and Knowledge Domains

**Research methodology.** The specialist understands when to use qualitative vs quantitative methods, how to design each correctly, and what each can and cannot tell you. They know that a survey cannot explain *why* something is true; only a qual method can. They know that an interview of twelve people cannot be extrapolated to a market. Knowing the limits of each method is as important as knowing how to execute them.

**Survey design.** Question wording, response scale selection (Likert, NPS, MaxDiff, conjoint), order effects, screener logic, sample size calculation, and panel selection. A poorly worded question produces garbage data. Survey design is a craft.

**Qualitative research.** Discussion guide construction, in-depth interview moderation, focus group facilitation, thematic coding of transcripts, and avoiding leading or confirmation-biased analysis. The skill is hearing what the respondent is actually saying, not what you hoped they'd say.

**Market sizing.** Top-down (TAM via industry reports), bottom-up (SAM/SOM via unit economics and target criteria), and triangulation between the two. Understanding where the numbers come from and what assumptions they rest on — not just reporting a figure.

**Audience segmentation.** Demographic, psychographic, behavioural, and needs-based segmentation. Knowing that job title is a weak segmentation variable; that attitudinal and motivational clusters are stronger; and that a segmentation is only useful if it's actionable for the team receiving it.

**Demand signal interpretation.** Reading search volume trends, category growth data, and consumer behaviour data as indicators of latent or emerging demand — distinct from web analytics or SEO work. The boundary: this specialist interprets what demand signals mean for market strategy, not for content or search optimisation. They may pull Google Trends data but they are not an SEO specialist.

**Statistical literacy.** Sufficient command of significance testing, confidence intervals, margin of error, and cross-tab analysis to know when a finding is real and when it's noise. Not a statistician — but not innumerate. Excel and basic SPSS/PSPP or Google Sheets pivot competency is baseline; advanced modelling is a bonus.

**Research ethics and study design integrity.** Informed consent, participant anonymity, non-deceptive recruitment, avoiding leading questions, and not cherry-picking data to confirm a prior belief. These are not optional courtesies — they are what makes the research valid.

## 3. Research Methodologies

**Quantitative — primary.**
- Online surveys (consumer panels, customer lists, LinkedIn audience targeting for B2B)
- Structured interviews with closed-ended questions (less common, used when phone/video is required)
- Conjoint analysis and MaxDiff for preference quantification
- A/B concept testing at scale

**Quantitative — secondary.**
- Syndicated market reports (Mintel, Euromonitor, IBISWorld, Forrester, Gartner)
- Government and statistical bureau data (ABS, Census, ONS, Eurostat, BLS)
- Industry association reports and trade data
- Platform-level demand data (Google Trends, keyword volume as proxy for category interest)

**Qualitative — primary.**
- Semi-structured in-depth interviews (IDIs) — 30–60 min, exploration of motivations, pain points, decision processes
- Focus groups — 6–10 participants, concept testing, message resonance, early ideation
- Ethnographic or observational research (less common, but used for behavioural questions)
- Diary studies / longitudinal qual (used for purchase journey or usage research)

**Qualitative — secondary.**
- Review mining (Amazon, G2, App Store, Trustpilot — extracting voice-of-customer language)
- Social listening (not the same as social media management — used here for category sentiment and emerging conversation themes)
- Forum and community analysis (Reddit, industry Slack communities, niche forums)

**Synthesis methods.**
- Thematic coding of interview transcripts
- Affinity mapping / clustering of qualitative insights
- Persona development from segmentation data
- Jobs-to-be-done framing for need identification

**B2B vs B2C note.** The methods are largely the same but the execution differs. B2B research involves smaller, harder-to-reach samples, longer sales cycles, multiple decision-makers (economic buyer, technical buyer, champion), and higher incentive requirements for participation. B2C research involves larger panels, faster fielding, and consumer panel platforms. A generalist specialist is comfortable in both contexts.

## 4. Tools and Platforms

**Survey and primary research.**
- Qualtrics — enterprise survey platform, advanced logic, panel integration
- SurveyMonkey / Typeform — lighter-weight surveys, faster deployment
- Prolific / Respondent.io — B2C and B2B panel recruitment for primary research
- Dscout — mobile diary studies and qual research
- UserZoom / Maze — qual/concept testing (overlaps with UX research; the market researcher uses these for concept validation, not usability)

**Secondary research and data sources.**
- IBISWorld, Mintel, Euromonitor — industry and market reports
- Statista — aggregated statistics from multiple sources (useful for quick data pulls; treat figures with appropriate scepticism and trace to primary source)
- Forrester, Gartner — B2B technology and enterprise market reports
- Factiva / Nexis — news and industry press monitoring
- LinkedIn Sales Navigator — B2B audience sizing and segmentation data

**Demand signal tools.**
- Google Trends — category and topic demand over time
- SEMrush / Ahrefs — keyword volume as demand proxy (note: used here for market insight, not SEO optimisation)
- Exploding Topics — emerging trend identification

**Analysis and synthesis.**
- Excel / Google Sheets — cross-tabs, pivot tables, basic statistical analysis
- SPSS or PSPP — for larger quantitative datasets requiring significance testing
- Dovetail — qualitative research repository and tagging
- Notion / Confluence — research documentation and findings libraries
- Miro / FigJam — affinity mapping, insight clustering

**Presentation and delivery.**
- Google Slides / PowerPoint — research decks for stakeholder presentation
- Canva — lightweight visual synthesis for internal briefs

## 5. Key Deliverables

- **Market sizing report** — TAM/SAM/SOM analysis with documented assumptions, data sources, and confidence ranges. Not a single number — a structured argument.
- **Audience persona document** — data-grounded personas built from segmentation research and qual interviews. Not creative profiles; analytical outputs with behavioural and attitudinal grounding.
- **Research brief / findings report** — synthesised output from a primary or secondary research project. Includes methodology, key findings, strategic implications, and recommended next actions.
- **Survey results deck** — visual presentation of quantitative findings, cross-tabs, and significant differences across segments.
- **Trend report** — synthesis of category dynamics, emerging signals, and directional indicators relevant to a market or audience segment.
- **Voice-of-customer (VoC) synthesis** — distilled verbatim language, recurring themes, and need clusters from qualitative fieldwork. Often feeds into messaging and positioning work.
- **Segmentation framework** — classification system for the target audience, with profiles of each segment, sizing where possible, and strategic relevance.
- **Research repository entry** — tagging and archiving of completed research for organisational reuse.

## 6. Cross-Functional Collaboration

**With Marketing.**
The most frequent relationship. Market Research feeds audience insight (personas, segment profiles, message resonance findings) to brand and content teams. Campaign briefs are stronger when grounded in real audience data. The researcher's job is to give marketers a picture of the audience that's truer than their assumptions.

**With Product.**
Market Research validates whether a problem is real and whether there is sufficient demand before product invests in building. Jobs-to-be-done research, concept testing, and unmet-needs analysis are the primary touchpoints. The researcher is not a UX researcher (that's a different role) but there is natural overlap on qual methods.

**With Strategy.**
Market sizing, industry trend analysis, and white-space identification inform strategic planning and investment cases. The researcher may be embedded in a strategy or planning cycle, delivering TAM analysis or horizon scanning.

**With Sales.**
ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) validation, buyer journey research, and understanding why deals are won or lost. Win/loss analysis is a related discipline — the Market Research Specialist may run buyer interviews as part of this work, though dedicated CI/sales intelligence roles often own competitive win/loss.

**With Leadership / Exec.**
Market Research is often called on to underpin business cases, board presentations, and funding narratives. The specialist needs to be able to present findings credibly to a non-research audience and field questions about methodology.

**Tension to be aware of.** Stakeholders frequently want research to confirm a decision already made. The researcher's job is to produce accurate findings, not to justify. This creates friction. Great researchers hold the line; mediocre ones let the brief drift toward advocacy.

## 7. What Distinguishes a Great Market Researcher from a Mediocre One

**Great researchers translate data into decisions.** The brief doesn't end with "here's what we found." It ends with "here's what we recommend you do, and here's why the data supports it." Mediocre researchers present findings and wait to be told what to do with them.

**Great researchers scope before they field.** They push back on vague briefs, insist on a clear research question, and resist the temptation to throw a survey at an ambiguous problem. Mediocre researchers start fieldwork before the question is properly defined and produce data that doesn't answer anything.

**Great researchers know the limits of their data.** They flag sample size constraints, acknowledge when a finding is directional rather than conclusive, and resist overstating certainty. Mediocre researchers present every finding with equal confidence.

**Great researchers hear what respondents are actually saying.** In qualitative work especially, they separate the stated reason from the real reason, and they don't project their own framework onto the data. This takes practice and intellectual humility.

**Great researchers maintain methodological rigour under commercial pressure.** When a stakeholder wants to cut the sample size to save budget, or reword a question to get a more favourable result, the researcher pushes back with a clear explanation of what that costs in validity.

**Great researchers build institutional memory.** They tag, document, and archive research so it can be reused. They know what's already been studied. They don't reinvent fieldwork that was done 18 months ago.

## 8. Constraints and Ethical Boundaries

**Informed consent.** Participants in primary research must understand what they're participating in, how their data will be used, and that participation is voluntary. This applies to surveys, interviews, and focus groups. Recruiting participants under false pretences (deceptive recruitment) is an ethical violation and, in many jurisdictions, a legal one.

**Anonymity and confidentiality.** Individual responses should be protected from identification unless participants have explicitly consented to attribution. Reporting that could identify specific respondents in a small sample requires care.

**Non-leading question design.** Questions must be designed to elicit genuine responses, not to nudge toward a preferred answer. Leading questions produce invalid data. Double-barreled questions produce uninterpretable data. This is both a quality issue and an ethics issue.

**Avoiding confirmation bias in synthesis.** The researcher must not cherry-pick findings that support a pre-existing hypothesis and ignore contradictory data. All significant findings — including inconvenient ones — must be reported.

**Data privacy compliance.** Primary research involving personal data triggers obligations under GDPR (EU/UK), CCPA (California), and equivalent frameworks elsewhere. This includes: having a lawful basis for collection, storage limitations, right-to-erasure obligations, and restrictions on using personal data for purposes beyond those originally disclosed. The researcher should not be the organisation's privacy lawyer, but they need to know when to escalate to one.

**Scope boundaries (role-specific).**
- This role does not produce competitive intelligence on named competitors.
- This role does not own SEO strategy or search optimisation.
- This role does not own UX research (usability testing, accessibility) — although there is method overlap.
- This role does not own analytics or data science — they consume and interpret, but do not build data infrastructure.

## Uncertainty Flags

- Tool list reflects common platforms as of 2025–2026. Specific platforms vary significantly by organisation size and budget. The list is illustrative, not exhaustive.
- The B2B vs B2C distinction is genuine but the boundary in practice is fuzzy — many generalist researchers do both within the same role.
- "Demand signal interpretation" is an area where the market researcher's remit and the SEO/analytics specialist's remit can overlap. The brief draws the boundary at strategic interpretation vs optimisation application — but Harper should be aware this may need clarification in the persona's scope definition.
- Regulatory specifics (GDPR, CCPA article numbers, etc.) are not cited here — they are subject to change and jurisdiction-specific. The ethical principles above are durable; the compliance detail should be treated as "escalate to legal/privacy counsel when in doubt."
